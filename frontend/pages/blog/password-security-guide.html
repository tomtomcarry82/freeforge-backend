Password Security Guide: The 7 Habits That Actually Keep Your Accounts Safe

By the FreeForge team · September 2026 · 7 min read

Most password advice you grew up with is outdated. The "must contain uppercase, a number, a symbol, and the blood of a unicorn" rules? They made passwords harder for you to remember while barely slowing down attackers. Meanwhile the things that actually get accounts hacked — password reuse, phishing, and leaked databases — barely get mentioned. Let's fix that.

1. Use a password manager — this is the single biggest upgrade

If you take one thing from this guide, take this. A password manager (Bitwarden, 1Password, or even the one built into your browser) generates and remembers a unique, random 20-character password for every site. You remember one strong master password; it remembers the other 200. Reused passwords are how most "hacks" actually happen — one site leaks, attackers try the same email/password everywhere else, and everything falls. A manager kills that attack completely.

2. Never reuse passwords across important accounts

Your email is the master key to everything — password resets flow through it. If your email password is also your forum password, your Netflix password, and your bank-adjacent password, one leak anywhere compromises everything. At minimum: email, banking, and anything tied to money get unique passwords. Ideally: everything gets a unique password, which is trivial once you have a manager.

3. Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA) where it matters

2FA means a stolen password alone isn't enough — the attacker also needs your phone or security key. Prioritize: email, bank/finance, cloud storage, social media, and anything work-related. Authenticator apps (or hardware keys like YubiKey) beat SMS codes, because SMS can be intercepted via SIM-swapping. SMS 2FA is still far better than no 2FA, though — don't let perfect be the enemy of good.

4. Use passphrases for the few passwords you must memorize

Your manager's master password and your device PIN are the only passwords your brain needs to hold. For those, use a passphrase: four or five random words strung together, like correct-horse-battery-staple (but make your own — don't use that one). Long and memorable beats short and cryptic. A 25-character passphrase of random words is both easier to type and harder to crack than P@ssw0rd!2024.

5. Check whether your passwords have already leaked

Billions of credentials are floating around in breach databases. Go to haveibeenpwned.com and enter your email — if it shows breaches, change those passwords now (and anywhere you reused them). This takes two minutes and it's free. Do it for every email address you own.

6. Learn to spot phishing — it's the #1 way passwords get stolen

Attackers rarely "hack" passwords anymore; they trick you into handing them over. Red flags: urgent language ("your account will be suspended in 24 hours"), slightly-off sender addresses, links that don't match the real domain when you hover. The defense is boring but effective: never log in from a link in an email or message. Open the site yourself by typing the address. And when in doubt, check with the company through their official support channel.

7. Save your backup/recovery codes somewhere safe

When you enable 2FA, most services give you backup codes. Screenshot them, print them, or store them in your password manager — somewhere other than the phone that holds your authenticator app. Lose your phone without backup codes and you'll discover what a "lengthy identity verification process" really feels like.

The stuff you can stop worrying about

  • Changing passwords every 90 days. Forced rotation just makes people pick weaker passwords (Password1 → Password2). Change a password when there's a reason: a breach, a shared computer, or a phishing scare.
  • Maximum complexity rules. Length beats complexity. A long passphrase outperforms an 8-character symbol salad.
  • Security questions. "Mother's maiden name" is public information in the age of social media. Treat security questions as second passwords — answer them with random strings stored in your manager.

Your 20-minute action plan

  1. Install a password manager and set a strong master passphrase (10 min).
  2. Change your email password to a generated one, then enable 2FA (5 min).
  3. Check haveibeenpwned.com and change any leaked passwords (5 min).

Do that this weekend and you'll be more secure than roughly 95% of internet users. The rest — rotating the long tail of old accounts — can happen gradually as you log into each one. Security isn't about being perfect; it's about being a harder target than the next person.
